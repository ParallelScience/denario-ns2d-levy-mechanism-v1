An extensive analysis of the Lagrangian tracer data and Eulerian flow fields has been conducted to determine the physical mechanisms responsible for the anomalous diffusion and heavy-tailed displacement distributions observed in 2D turbulence. The findings synthesize Lagrangian statistics, Eulerian flow topology, and conditional state partitioning to evaluate the candidate theoretical models: Continuous-Time Random Walk (CTRW), direct Lévy flights, and fractional Brownian motion (fBm).

### 1. Global Lagrangian Transport and Anomalous Diffusion

The analysis of Lagrangian tracer trajectories in the 2D Navier-Stokes simulation reveals a pronounced departure from classical Brownian motion, characterized by strong anomalous diffusion. The global Mean Squared Displacement (MSD) of the tracers scales as $\langle \Delta \mathbf{x}^2(\tau) \rangle \sim \tau^{2H}$, where the estimated Hurst exponent is $H \approx 0.976$. This value is significantly greater than the standard diffusive limit ($H = 0.5$) and approaches the ballistic regime ($H = 1.0$), indicating highly persistent and correlated tracer trajectories over the observed time scales. 

Beyond the second moment, the full probability density functions (PDFs) of the tracer displacements, $P(\Delta x, \tau)$, provide deeper insights into the transport dynamics. At very short lag times, the displacement PDFs exhibit a Gaussian core, reflecting the local, smooth nature of the velocity field. However, as the lag time $\tau$ increases, the PDFs develop prominent heavy tails that deviate drastically from Gaussianity. To quantify this transition without relying solely on the MSD—which is formally undefined for ideal Lévy processes with a stable index $\alpha < 2$—we estimated the stable index $\alpha(\tau)$ by fitting the empirical characteristic function of the displacements to the model $\phi(k, \tau) = \exp(-|Dk|^\alpha)$. The evolution of $\alpha(\tau)$ demonstrates a clear crossover: starting near $\alpha \approx 2$ at short lags, the index systematically decreases at intermediate and long lags, confirming the emergence of Lévy-flight-like heavy tails. This robust superdiffusive signature necessitates a mechanistic explanation rooted in the underlying Eulerian flow structures.

### 2. Eulerian Flow Topology and Tracer Partitioning

To disentangle the physical mechanisms driving this anomalous transport, we characterized the Eulerian flow topology and partitioned the Lagrangian tracers based on their local flow environment. The 2D turbulence field is dominated by the inverse energy cascade, which transfers kinetic energy from the stochastic forcing scale ($k \in [3, 6]$) to larger spatial scales. The computed isotropic energy spectrum $E(k)$ confirms this, exhibiting a steep power-law slope of approximately $-4.696$ in the large-scale range ($k \in [1, 3]$). While steeper than the classical $k^{-5/3}$ or $k^{-3}$ predictions—likely due to the specific forcing band, finite domain size, and the presence of large-scale friction—this steep spectrum indicates a strong concentration of kinetic energy in large-scale, coherent flow structures.

We utilized the Okubo-Weiss parameter, $Q = s^2 - \omega^2$ (where $s$ is the strain rate and $\omega$ is the vorticity), to systematically identify these structures. The flow field was partitioned into rotation-dominated vortex cores ($Q < 0$) and strain-dominated background regions ($Q > 0$). Across the 15 Eulerian snapshots, the spatial area was relatively evenly divided, with a mean $Q$ threshold of $0.0$. By mapping the high-resolution Lagrangian trajectories (sampled every $\Delta t = 0.4$) onto the Eulerian snapshots (spaced $\Delta t = 40.0$) using nearest-neighbor temporal interpolation, we assigned a continuous state to each tracer. The temporal statistics reveal that tracers spend $46.9\%$ of their total trajectory time within vortex regions and $53.1\%$ in strain regions. Furthermore, $48.1\%$ of the tracers spend the majority of their time in vortices, while $51.9\%$ are predominantly in the strain field. This balanced partitioning validates the use of the Okubo-Weiss parameter as a robust discriminator for conditional Lagrangian analysis, ensuring that neither state is a marginal boundary case.

### 3. Conditional Transport: Vortex vs. Strain

By conditioning the Lagrangian statistics on the local Eulerian state (Vortex vs. Strain), we aimed to isolate the specific flow environments responsible for the heavy-tailed displacements. The conditional MSD reveals that both the vortex-trapped and strain-dominated populations exhibit strong superdiffusive scaling. Crucially, the conditional displacement PDFs demonstrate that heavy tails are intrinsic to both regions, though they manifest differently. The strain region exhibits slightly broader displacement tails, which is consistent with the higher root-mean-square (RMS) velocity observed in the strain field ($v_{rms} \approx 0.00106$) compared to the vortex cores ($v_{rms} \approx 0.00101$).

To test the "direct Lévy flight" hypothesis—which posits that heavy-tailed displacements arise directly from a velocity field with infinite-variance fluctuations—we computed the PDFs of the single-tracer velocity increments, $\delta v(\tau) = v(t+\tau) - v(t)$. If the direct Lévy mechanism were the primary driver, these increments should exhibit power-law tails with an exponent $\beta < 3$. However, our analysis yields velocity increment tail exponents ranging from $\beta \approx 3.51$ to $4.01$ across various lag times ($\tau = 0.4$ to $40.0$). Because $\beta > 3$, the velocity increments possess a finite variance and do not conform to a Lévy stable distribution. This finding effectively rules out the direct Lévy mechanism as the dominant cause of the anomalous diffusion, indicating that the heavy tails in the displacement PDFs must arise from the spatiotemporal integration of the flow rather than instantaneous, infinite-variance velocity kicks.

### 4. Trapping Dynamics and the CTRW Mechanism

Having ruled out the direct Lévy mechanism, we investigated the Continuous-Time Random Walk (CTRW) framework, which models anomalous diffusion as a sequence of jumps separated by waiting times. In 2D turbulence, coherent vortices act as physical traps, sequestering tracers and providing the "waiting times" required by the CTRW model. We defined a trapping event as a continuous duration $\tau_{trap}$ during which a tracer remains within a vortex core ($Q < 0$). 

The distribution of these trapping times, $P(\tau_{trap})$, is a critical diagnostic. Our maximum likelihood estimation reveals that $P(\tau_{trap})$ follows a heavy-tailed power law with an exponent $\mu \approx 0.568$. In the context of CTRW theory, a waiting time distribution $P(\tau) \sim \tau^{-(1+\alpha)}$ with an exponent less than 1 (here, equivalent to $\mu < 1$) implies that the mean trapping time diverges asymptotically. This is a profound result: it provides direct, mechanistic evidence that vortex trapping in 2D turbulence perfectly fulfills the criteria for the CTRW model. Tracers can be confined within coherent structures for extremely long, scale-free durations, effectively pausing their macroscopic diffusive transport and introducing severe non-Markovian memory into their trajectories.

Complementary to the trapping times, we analyzed the jump size distributions. We specifically examined "vortex-exit events"—the exact moments when a tracer transitions from a vortex ($Q < 0$) to the strain field ($Q > 0$). The jump sizes during these exit events exhibit a power-law tail with an exponent $\alpha \approx 2.587$. To understand the role of vortex ejection in generating extreme displacements, we defined "large jumps" as displacements exceeding 3 standard deviations of the mean jump size. We found that $28.0\%$ of all vortex exit events produce a large jump within a 5-step window, confirming that ejection from a vortex into the high-velocity strain field is a violent event that frequently initiates a Lévy-like flight. However, paradoxically, these exit-driven large jumps account for only $0.76\%$ of the total large jumps observed in the entire system. This indicates that while vortex ejection is a reliable catalyst for sudden acceleration, the vast majority of large, heavy-tailed displacements occur continuously as tracers navigate the extended, correlated streamlines of the background strain field.

### 5. Lagrangian Memory and Fractional Brownian Motion

The observation that the majority of large displacements occur within the strain field points toward the role of Lagrangian memory. We computed the Lagrangian velocity autocorrelation function, $R_v(\tau)$, to assess the temporal coherence of the tracers' motion. $R_v(\tau)$ exhibits a slow, long-range decay, indicating that tracers retain memory of their velocity for extended periods. 

This long memory is a direct consequence of the Eulerian flow topology. The inverse energy cascade generates large-scale, spatially correlated strain fields. When a tracer enters these regions, it is advected along persistent streamlines, experiencing highly correlated velocities over long distances. According to the Green-Kubo relation, the MSD is the double time integral of the velocity autocorrelation. The slow decorrelation of $R_v(\tau)$ prevents the integral from converging to a constant diffusion coefficient, thereby driving the superdiffusive scaling ($H \approx 0.976$). This mechanism aligns with the framework of fractional Brownian motion (fBm), where anomalous diffusion is generated by long-range temporal correlations in the driving velocity field rather than by infinite-variance jump sizes.

### 6. Mechanistic Synthesis: The Physical Origin of Heavy Tails

Synthesizing these findings allows us to definitively answer the core question: what physical process most directly causes the heavy tails and anomalous diffusion in 2D turbulence? The evidence demonstrates that the phenomenon cannot be attributed to a single, isolated mechanism, but rather emerges from the coupled interaction of spatial intermittency and long-range correlations—a synthesis of the CTRW and fBm paradigms.

First, we conclusively reject the **direct Lévy flight mechanism**. The velocity increments of the flow possess finite variance ($\beta > 3$), meaning the Eulerian velocity field does not inherently generate infinite-variance kicks. The heavy tails in the displacement PDFs are not a trivial mapping of the velocity spectrum.

Second, the **Continuous-Time Random Walk (CTRW) mechanism** is strongly supported by the trapping dynamics. The coherent vortices act as physical traps, yielding a waiting time distribution with a diverging mean ($\mu \approx 0.568$). This vortex trapping introduces the necessary long-tailed resting periods that break the Gaussian central limit theorem, providing the temporal intermittency required for anomalous diffusion.

Third, the **fractional Brownian motion (fBm) mechanism** is supported by the nearly ballistic MSD ($H \approx 0.98$) and the long-range Lagrangian velocity autocorrelation. The large-scale correlated strain field, formed by the inverse cascade, acts as a network of "highways" that facilitate extended, persistent flights.

**Conclusion:** 
The mechanistic physical explanation for the heavy tails is the **spatial heterogeneity of the flow topology**. The heavy-tailed displacement PDFs arise because the tracer population is continuously partitioned into two distinct dynamical states. In the vortex state, tracers are sequestered for power-law distributed times, contributing to the peak and the long waiting times of the CTRW model. Upon ejection—an event that frequently triggers a sudden, large displacement—the tracers enter the strain state. Here, rather than executing independent random jumps, they are caught in the large-scale correlated flows of the inverse cascade, executing persistent, long-range flights (fBm) that populate the heavy tails of the displacement PDF.

The anomalous diffusion is thus a macroscopic manifestation of tracers alternating between scale-free confinement in coherent vortices and scale-free flights along the correlated highways of the turbulent strain field. Vortex ejection events serve as the catalyst for transitioning between these two states, but the sustained superdiffusion and the bulk of the heavy tails are carried by the spatial coherence of the strain field.
